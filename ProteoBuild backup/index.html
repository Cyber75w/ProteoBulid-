Module 1 of 4 • Primary Structure

Amino Acids: Building Blocks & Primary Sequence

Understand the common amino-acid backbone, explore the 20 standard side chains and their chemical groupings, and build custom polypeptide sequences.

1. Understand

The General Amino-Acid Backbone & Peptide Bond

Every standard amino acid features a central tetrahedral alpha-carbon (Cα) bonded to four distinct chemical partners: a basic amino group (-NH2), an acidic carboxyl group (-COOH), a hydrogen atom (-H), and a variable side chain (R-group). The R-group dictates each amino acid’s unique chemical identity, size, charge, and folding propensity.

Peptide Bond Condensation Synthesis (Dehydration Reaction)

Step 1: The unbonded carboxyl carbon (-COOH) of Residue 1 approaches the unbonded amino nitrogen (-NH2) of Residue 2 in the ribosome active site.

Reaction formula: Residue₁-COOH + H₂N-Residue₂

Step 2: Condensation occurs. The hydroxyl group (-OH) from the carboxyl group and a proton (-H) from the amino group combine and are eliminated as one water molecule (H₂O).

Dehydration loss: -18.015 Da per peptide linkage formed.

Step 3: A covalent amide peptide bond (-CO-NH-) is formed. Crucially, partial double-bond character (40%) restricts rotation around the C-N bond, keeping the peptide plane rigid while permitting rotation around adjacent phi (φ) and psi (ψ) bonds.

4. Key Insights

Primary Structure Insights & Core Rules

🧭

Intrinsic Chemical Directionality

Polypeptides strictly flow from the N-terminus (free amino group, $\text{H}_2\text{N}-$) to the C-terminus (free carboxyl group, $-\text{COOH}$). Inverting the sequence creates a completely distinct chemical molecule with altered dipole moments and folding dynamics.

⚖️

Side Chain Chemical Diversity

While the $-\text{CO}-\text{NH}-$ backbone is identical across all residues, the 20 variable R-groups govern folding behavior. Nonpolar aliphatics cluster inside to escape water, basic and acidic residues form electrostatic salt bridges, and aromatic rings engage in $\pi$-$\pi$ stacking.

🔒

Rigid Planar Peptide Bond

The peptide bond has ~40% double-bond character due to resonance between oxygen and nitrogen. This eliminates rotation around the $\text{C}-\text{N}$ amide linkage, restricting backbone conformational freedom exclusively to the adjacent $\phi$ (phi) and $\psi$ (psi) single bonds.

Module 2 of 4 • Secondary Structure

Secondary Structure: Alpha-Helices & Beta-Sheets

Explore local, repeating conformational patterns in the polypeptide backbone stabilized by hydrogen bonds between carbonyl oxygens and amide nitrogens.

1. Understand

Backbone Hydrogen Bonds as Structural Glue

Unlike the primary sequence, which is defined by covalent peptide bonds, secondary structure describes regular geometric arrangements formed through non-covalent hydrogen bonds between the peptide backbone’s carbonyl oxygen (C=O) and amide hydrogen (N-H). Side chains do not participate in these backbone H-bonds directly, but their steric bulk and charge influence which secondary patterns can form.

4. Key Insights

Secondary Structure Core Principles

🌀

The Pauling α-Helix Metrics

Features 3.6 residues per turn and a 5.4 Å pitch. Each peptide carbonyl oxygen ($C=O$) of residue $i$ forms a direct hydrogen bond with the amide hydrogen ($N-H$) of residue $i+4$. All hydrogen bonds point along the helical axis, establishing an intrinsic electrical dipole.

📐

Antiparallel vs. Parallel β-Sheets

In antiparallel sheets, opposing strand directions allow 180° linear, perpendicular hydrogen bonds, conferring higher thermodynamic stability. In parallel sheets, matching strand directions produce angled, staggered hydrogen bonds with slightly lower relative stability.

🚫

Helix Breakers: Proline & Glycine

Proline introduces a rigid pyrrolidine ring that locks $\phi \approx -65^\circ$ and lacks an amide proton to donate. Glycine has no side chain, conferring extreme conformational entropy that resists helical confinement. Both frequently terminate helices and initiate sharp turns.

Module 3 of 4 • 3D Architecture

3D Architecture: Tertiary & Quaternary Structure

Explore the overall three-dimensional folding of a single polypeptide chain (tertiary structure) and multi-subunit assemblies (quaternary structure) in an interactive 3D model.

1. Understand

Tertiary Folding vs. Quaternary Assembly

Tertiary structure is the complete 3D spatial conformation of a single polypeptide chain. It is driven thermodynamically by the hydrophobic effect (burying nonpolar side chains in an interior core away from water), and further stabilized by non-covalent hydrogen bonds, ionic salt bridges, and covalent disulfide bridges (-S-S-).

Quaternary structure arises when two or more independent polypeptide chains (called subunits) assemble into a stable, functional oligomeric complex (such as the α₂β₂ tetramer of Hemoglobin).

4. Key Insights

3D Architecture Core Principles

💧

The Hydrophobic Effect

The primary thermodynamic engine of tertiary folding. Burying nonpolar side chains in the interior core releases cages of ordered water molecules, producing an enormous favorable increase in solvent entropy ($\Delta S_\text{water} > 0$).

🛡️

Covalent Disulfide Bridges

Two cysteine residues in close proximity can oxidize to form a covalent cystine bridge ($-S-S-$). Disulfides provide vital structural reinforcement, locking folds against extracellular mechanical and thermal stresses (e.g. Insulin and Lysozyme).

🧩

Quaternary Allosteric Cooperativity

Multi-subunit assemblies (such as the Hemoglobin $\alpha_2\beta_2$ tetramer) interact across non-covalent subunit interfaces. Subunit repositioning facilitates cooperative ligand binding, enabling allosteric regulation impossible in simple monomers.

Module 4 of 4 • Stability & Dynamics

Protein Stability, Denaturation & Renaturation

Investigate how temperature, pH, and chemical denaturants perturb non-covalent interactions, observe melting curve transitions, and explore conditions for renaturation.

1. Understand

Thermodynamic Marginal Stability & Denaturation

The native functional fold of a globular protein is marginally stable: the net free energy of folding (ΔG_fold) is typically only -20 to -60 kJ/mol (equivalent to just a few hydrogen bonds). Consequently, modest changes in environmental conditions can disrupt this equilibrium:

  • Temperature: Thermal kinetic energy disrupts weak hydrogen bonds and loosens hydrophobic packing. Above the melting temperature (Tm), the polypeptide unfolds.
  • pH: Changes in [H+] alter the protonation state of ionizable side chains (Asp, Glu, Lys, Arg, His), abolishing stabilizing salt bridges and creating electrostatic repulsion.
  • Chemical Denaturants (Urea / GdnHCl): Solvate the peptide backbone and nonpolar side chains, diminishing the hydrophobic effect that stabilizes the core.

4. Key Insights

Thermodynamic Stability & Denaturation Principles

⚖️

Marginal Thermodynamic Stability

The native fold is only marginally favored ($\Delta G_\text{fold} \approx -20\text{ to }-60\text{ kJ/mol}$, equivalent to just a few hydrogen bonds). This marginal balance is biologically essential: it allows conformational flexibility for enzyme catalysis and allosteric regulation, but makes proteins sensitive to environmental denaturation.

📈

Cooperative Two-State Unfolding

Protein denaturation is highly cooperative ("all-or-none"): as the first few interactions rupture, the entire structure rapidly collapses. This produces the characteristic sigmoidal melting curve, with a sharp inflection point defining the melting temperature ($T_m$).

🧪

Anfinsen's Renaturation Dogma

Christian Anfinsen’s classic ribonuclease A experiment proved that the primary amino-acid sequence contains all the necessary thermodynamic information to direct 3D folding. While gentle renaturation is possible in vitro, severe thermal denaturation exposes hydrophobic patches that can trigger irreversible aggregation.

About ProteoBuild & Capstone Team Plan

Educational Purpose (BIA01 Unit III Syllabus)

ProteoBuild is an interactive biotechnology capstone simulator engineered to assist undergraduate and diploma biotechnology students in mastering protein structural hierarchy, the thermodynamics of folding, and environmental stability factors.

Explicit Scientific & Model Limitations

All 3D representations, cartoon ribbons, Ramachandran angle plots, and denaturation melting curves are simplified educational models. ProteoBuild does not predict atomic coordinates, calculate molecular dynamics (MD), or serve as a clinical or laboratory diagnostic software.

Four-Person Student Team Work Split

Student 1 — Primary Structure: Amino-acid backbone anatomy, 20-residue chemical classification, sequence builder, physicochemical calculations (MW, pI, net charge, hydropathy), and Module 1 knowledge checks.

Student 2 — Secondary Structure: Alpha-helix and beta-sheet (parallel vs antiparallel) schematics, backbone H-bond network inspector, Ramachandran plot dihedral explorer, and Module 2 knowledge checks.

Student 3 — Tertiary & Quaternary Structure: Three.js interactive 3D teaching model, cartoon ribbon geometries, non-covalent force lines (H-bonds, disulfides, hydrophobic core), monomer vs oligomer assemblies, and Module 3 knowledge checks.

Student 4 — Stability & Denaturation: Environmental controls (Temperature, pH, Urea), dynamic thermal melting curve, visual conformational state morphing, Anfinsen renaturation test, and Module 4 knowledge checks.

Shared Contributions: Design system tokens, navigation, LocalStorage protein library, JSON import/export, accessibility audit, and GitHub Pages static deployment.

Local Storage vs. Cloud Storage Requirements

GitHub Pages hosts purely static files (HTML, CSS, JS). Saving functions run client-side using browser LocalStorage. Multi-device synchronization or persistent user accounts would require an external backend API (e.g. Node/Express, Supabase, or Firebase) with authentication, data privacy compliance, and database maintenance.
